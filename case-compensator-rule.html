Evidence, case 2, the compensator rule and the loss model

The rule was right for one phase. It was used for four.

A published compensator design rule, twenty years old and widely taught, reproduced from its own figures and found to be incomplete for the multiphase case it is used in. The gap was derived before the source text was read, then confirmed at two, four and eight phases against one simulated and one measured arbiter, with the alternative explanation stated in advance, tested, and refuted. The loss model for the next design step was then predicted, measured, and measured again after its own measuring script was found to be wrong.

Provenance

A 2004 doctoral dissertation on multiphase voltage-regulator control. Public, citable, and still in use. A commercial circuit simulator, stock install. One engineer. No client material of any kind is involved in this case.

Everything here is reproducible by you. The source states its component values, its switching frequencies, its target bandwidth and its output-impedance rule, and it prints a measured loop-gain plot with a marker on it. That is why this design was chosen over a current one. A modern application note gives you a topology and no arbiter, and grading your own homework is not evidence.

What was found

The published rule sets the compensator's DC gain from the current-sense gain and the output capacitor. Built exactly as printed, on the author's own two-phase ceramic design, it lands at 2.18 times its own stated bandwidth and half its own stated output impedance.

Divided by the phase count, the same design lands on both.

The four-phase board's switching frequency is not stated in the source, so it was swept. Three decks sat inside the model's own guard, a crossover below a fifth of the switching frequency, declared before the runs. The best of them lands at +0.08 % and the worst at +3.01 %.

Two phases was never going to be enough, and neither was four. A factor of two is the least diagnostic number in engineering. It is equally consistent with a transcription slip, a peak-versus-average confusion, or a single-ended versus differential sense, and none of those has anything to do with phase count. Four separates those. Four does not separate "the divisor is the phase count" from "the divisor saturates at four".

So that alternative was written down as the interesting outcome, before the deck existed, in the file that specifies the experiment. The file calls a result near 4 "the interesting one" and says it would narrow the published claim to "up to four phases", with every sentence of the finding rewritten.

It came back 7.95063. The alternative is refuted.

The prediction that failed

MEASURED A sharper companion prediction said the negative bias was systematic and would put the eight-phase result between 7.93 and 7.95. It measured 7.95063, six ten-thousandths outside the band. FAIL Recorded as FAIL and left as written. It identified a real effect and missed its size. That is a different result from being wrong, and collapsing the two loses the distinction.

And the loop model behind it

MEASURED A closed-form model built from the author's own two equations reproduces the measured loop's magnitude to 0.03 dB from fs/20 to fs/5. With one term added, the output resistance that follows from the modulator's own definition, a single transport delay leaves a phase residual of 0.400 degrees RMS from 10 to 200 kHz. Above fs/4.1 it stops being a model of that loop, and the file states that limit beside the model so it is never quoted outside its range.

Stage 2: the loss model, checked twice

The corrected loop is the first half of the case. The second half is a power stage upgraded from 2004 devices to 2023 devices, and that needs a loss model: how conduction, gate charge, body-diode conduction during dead time and winding resistance divide the budget. The model was checked against the simulator, and then the check was checked.

What it says about modern silicon

MEASURED Dead time costs the same on modern silicon. Each 80 ns of dead time costs 2.24 efficiency points on the 2004 devices and 2.36 points on the 2023 devices. The body-diode drop is 0.8 V on both. The body diode's share of the loss budget:

At 100 ns on the modern devices the body diode is nearly half the entire budget. Upgrade the silicon and leave the dead time alone, and the loss moves toward the one term nobody touched.

Continuous conduction only, 4.4 A to 25 A.

Beyond the fitted range

The same discipline with nobody at the machine

Since 2026-09-18 the case has run on a loop on the engineer's own workstation: Windows Task Scheduler tasks running plain PowerShell scripts, a queue of job files, and Claude Code, Anthropic's command-line agent, running the Claude Opus 5 model for the jobs that read, derive and write. Every job ends in a receipt with its verdict, its inputs by hash and its outputs by hash. A pre-registration is hashed before the deck it judges exists. The simulator is started and driven by a script through the operating system's UI Automation interface. A governor job applies the engineer's current plan and stops when the next step needs his ruling.

MEASURED On 2026-09-21 the loop rebuilt one of Stage 2's corners from its script under a new name, ran it and read it: pre-registration at 13:30, simulation at 13:40, reading finished at 13:56. The new output file is 24,003,206 bytes and identical, byte for byte, to the one the same corner produced on 2026-09-11. The reading script printed the recorded values 21 of 21 and both readings 10 of 10.

The run shows that the route from a queued job to a measured number works with no one operating the machine, and that the simulator reproduced its own output exactly across ten days and a rebuilt deck. The rulings that shaped this case, including which reading of the second 1 MHz deck stands, were made by the engineer, and the loop carried them out. A session that runs unattended is evidence about throughput, and this page claims nothing more from it.

How it was found

By an operating discipline that the tooling made cheap enough to run every time.

The check is written before the thing it judges. Predictions, tolerances and decidability rules go into the file first. A check that cannot fail is not a check.
Every criterion carries a tier. A tier is never upgraded because a result looks right.
When a parameter cannot be obtained, the equation is inverted and the requirement stated. Never guessed.
A number from a summary is not a verified number. Every figure is re-read from the file that produced it before it is published, including on this page, where doing so caught a stale count one step before v2 shipped, a corner-voltage figure that was really a spread before v3 did, and before v4 a sentence on coupling that the case had already measured false.
A check's scope is part of the check. A parser that silently excludes what it was asked about must abstain. Zero files scanned and zero problems found print identically, so the count is part of the verdict.
Both sides of a comparison are read with one instrument. When the reading script was fixed, the prediction's band was re-derived with the fixed script before anything was read against it.
Typeset mathematics is read as an image, never trusted from text extraction.
A comparison that cannot separate the hypotheses is no evidence, and is reported as such.

Supporting this: schematics generated from scripts, so topology is right by construction; a netlist check that reads what the solver receives, which can differ from what was intended; a state walk over the switching period run before any simulation; and a design-sheet gate that refuses to emit a deck whose numbers disagree with the sheet that specified it.

Two pre-flights, quoted because they are the shape of the whole approach: 11,571 pin pairs agree, 0 disagree at four phases; 30,739 agree, 0 disagree at eight, each against an independently written netlist. MEASURED

Where the work was wrong

This list is where the page earns its credibility, and it is longer than the findings list on purpose. Each entry names what caught it.

Stage 1

A claim made to the engineer that was simply false. He was told the simulator was unavailable and two queued items were blocked, on the strength of a failure in a subsystem the simulator does not use. The project's own reference file already said that subsystem fails intermittently and said not to treat it as a blocker. It was hit, and reported as a blocker anyway. He caught it in one short sentence. The rule this produced: a capability is not unavailable until the mechanism it uses has been tried and failed.

A deck that was valid and was the wrong circuit. Two functions bound a phase count as a default argument, frozen at import, while a third read it at call time. An eight-phase request would have produced a four-phase deck that pre-flighted clean, ran, and got measured as eight: internally consistent, perfectly valid, and wrong. No checker that inspects one artefact can see that. It was caught by a check that compares two independently built descriptions, which named all 96 missing pins.

The same error three times in one script, in a single day. A derivation printed a table without checking it and published negative inductances as data. Fixing it produced a bound, which was then not applied to the table one section below, where every coupled row was outside it. The fix for that ends with the sentence "conflating the two would have been a third instance of the same error in one script." The third instance was committed two sections later. Naming a failure mode in writing does not stop you committing it.

A prediction that was pre-registered, fired as designed, and still tested the wrong statistic. It would have buried a refutation the data supports at 3.45 standard errors.

A loop gain read upside down. The export does not preserve the column order it was asked for. It was caught by an extractor that refuses anything but exactly one falling crossing. Nobody had noticed it. The same export did it again the same day on the eight-phase deck, and the fix held.

A verification gate that returned CLEAN on a file it never opened. Its file walk did not include the format the artefact was in.

A gate whose rulings depended on which directory you ran it from. A properly reviewed file reported as an unreviewed hit from anywhere else, which trains a reader to dismiss hits, the exact damage the gate exists to prevent.

A published figure that was wrong, and a correction that was worse. The ledger above tells it in full.

A question put to the engineer with the wrong evidence attached. The item was named correctly and the evidence shown belonged to a different item. He was told, because a person who rules on evidence is entitled to know when the evidence he was shown was not the evidence for the thing he ruled on, whether or not it changed his answer.

Stage 2 and the loop

A headline agreement that was two errors cancelling. Conduction loss "agreed to 0.018 %" because the top switch read 1.20 % high and the bottom 0.38 % low. The build record's own text had warned about exactly this kind of agreement a paragraph earlier.

A measuring script one sample early, which moved a pre-registered verdict twice. The error was about 3.6 times the width of the band it was judged against. It was found by writing the reader a second time, independently, and looking at why the two disagreed.

A deck with no body diode on its high side, left out on purpose with the reason written down. The reason was that positive inductor current keeps that diode reverse-biased. It held in the steady-state window every corner was read in, and nowhere else. With both switches off and the inductor current negative, at start-up, light load or discontinuous conduction, the switch node had only the switch's 1 MOhm off-resistance to push against, which is the 30 kV spike at the start of one trace. The steady-state readings at 25 A are unchanged by construction, so the figures above stand. The fix is a mirrored diode per phase, and the switching-state check now walks it.

A step reported as cancelled that had run. The interface showed a delivery step as cancelled; on the machine it had run in full and installed a file. The next delivery's name check found that file already in place and stopped before anything was sent twice. An interrupted step is now treated as unknown until its possible outputs have been inspected.

An isolation check that held while the answers crossed. If you are designing a modern multiphase rail

This guideline had twenty years of scrutiny. Yours has had two.

The finding concerns per-phase sensed current into a shared output capacitor, the control structure that a discrete multiphase buck, an inverse-coupled build and a trans-inductor voltage regulator all share unchanged. Magnetics do not enter it. The topology moved, and the loop architecture stayed where it was.

So the obvious question was put to the work: does the correction survive coupling?

MEASURED It does. The author's two-phase ceramic design was built again with its two inductors inversely coupled at α = -0.5, and the corrected and as-printed decks were compared within each condition. Their output-impedance ratio measured 1.95465 coupled against 1.97642 uncoupled, 1.10 % apart. Had coupling collapsed the divisor, the ratio would have fallen to 1.00, a factor of two away, and that outcome was written down before the decks were built. Read a second way, as the droop after a load step, which a voltmeter can check, the ratio measured 1.99024 coupled against 1.99174 uncoupled, 0.075 % apart. Coupling redistributes di/dt between phases and leaves the count of phases delivering into the bank alone, and a mutual inductance passes no DC. The divisor is the number of phases feeding the capacitor bank, however they are wound.

The section's second claim was measured false. Earlier drafts of this page said, tagged DERIVED, that what coupling moves is each phase's finite output resistance, in step with the differential inductance. At α = -0.5 that inductance is 1.5 times the uncoupled one, so the resistance should have risen 1.5 times and the loop gain with it. MEASURED At two loads and two compensator settings, coupling lowered the DC loop gain, by 0.22 to 0.55 dB in four pairs of decks out of four, and the drop grew with the load by 1.735 and 1.769 against the 1.668 registered for a change at the output node. DERIVED Read through the plant model, the output resistance moved by a factor of 0.824, softer where the claim said stiffer, with a 6.6 % spread across four extractions, and neither eigenvalue of the coupled inductance accounts for it. At α = -0.5 on a two-phase cell, inverse coupling lowers the DC loop gain slightly and leaves the closed-loop droop unchanged to better than 0.1 %. Its case rests on ripple and transient behaviour.

And checking that algebra turned up a bound nobody had written down. For a symmetric coupled group to describe a physical magnetic circuit, the coupling coefficient is limited by the group size: at four coupled phases it cannot go below -0.333, at eight below -0.143. Vendor application notes report coupled groups of two and three, an interleaving rule that divides by the group size, and ripple that rose when a third phase joined. They report the practice, and the bound explains it.

NOT EVALUABLE The trans-inductor case stays open, now for a stated reason. Its series secondary adds a component common to every sensed current, where coupling redistributes among them, and that path's gain has not been derived.

The session that produced the coupling measurement was recorded, and the cut of it is on the Evidence page. It ends on that number.

What transfers

None of this is specific to one simulator or one converter.

  • Predictions committed before runs, with tolerances and a stated failure branch
  • Claim tiering, with NOT EVALUABLE as a first-class result
  • Inverted requirements in place of guessed parameters
  • Artefacts generated from scripts, checked against an independently written description of the same thing
  • Identifiers derived from the artefact on every use, never carried between runs
  • One instrument on both sides of every comparison, and a second, independent instrument to test the first
  • Gates validated by firing them on known-bad input, every time, because a gate that has never failed has never been tested
  • Every figure re-derived from its source file before publication

The discipline is the portable asset. The tooling makes it affordable to run, and the engineer still decides.

One engineer, a public source, and a simulator you already own.

If that produced a correction to a rule your field has used for twenty years, confirmed across a factor of four in phase count, it is worth an hour to talk about what the same discipline finds in your flow.
